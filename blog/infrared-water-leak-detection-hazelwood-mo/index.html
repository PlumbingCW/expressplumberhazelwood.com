Infrared Water Leak Detection: What It Can and Cannot Find.

Infrared leak detection uses a thermal camera to show temperature differences on a wall, ceiling or floor. A wet area is usually a different temperature than the dry surface around it, so a cool or warm patch can point to a leak you cannot see. It is a screening tool, not proof. A plumber still has to confirm the source before cutting into anything.

Here is how the method works, where it helps, where it fails and what other tests back it up. If someone offers infrared as a guaranteed answer, ask what they will do to confirm it.

How a thermal camera sees a leak.

A thermal camera does not see water. It sees surface temperature. Evaporating water cools a surface, so a damp patch of drywall often shows up as a darker area on the screen. A hot water line leaking into a wall can show the opposite, a warm streak.

The camera works best when there is a clear contrast. A leak behind a plain interior wall in a heated room is a good case. A thick concrete slab, a cold basement wall or direct sunlight on the surface makes the picture harder to read.

What a plumber does around the camera.

  1. Ask what you have noticed. Stains, a musty smell, the sound of running water, a spinning meter or an odd bill are all clues.
  2. Run a meter check. With every fixture off, a moving meter dial suggests a leak somewhere on the property.
  3. Scan with the thermal camera. The plumber sweeps likely walls, ceilings and floors and notes anything that looks off.
  4. Confirm with a moisture meter. A probe reads moisture in drywall or wood, which tells you whether a cool patch is actually wet.
  5. Use other tools if needed. Pressure tests, acoustic listening and a camera in the drain help pin down the exact spot.

The last step is why infrared is only one piece. A plumber who opens a wall based only on a thermal image can cut in the wrong place.

Honest limits you should know.

  • It cannot tell a leak from condensation, a sweating pipe or a humid corner without a second test.
  • It does not see through metal ductwork, thick tile or a concrete slab well.
  • It shows a surface, so a leak deep inside a wall may only show a faint pattern.
  • It cannot date a leak. A stain may be from a repair that was already done.
  • Results change with room temperature, season and how long the leak has been running.

That is not a reason to skip infrared. It is a fast, non-invasive first look that can save a wall from needless cuts. Treat it as a clue, then confirm.

Local note

Hazelwood winter conditions that cause hidden leaks.

The City of Hazelwood lists a frost depth of 30 inches and a winter design temperature of 2 degrees F. Pipes in exterior walls, crawl spaces and unheated garages can freeze and split, and the leak often shows only after a thaw. Missouri American Water crews handled 16 main breaks in one day during the January 2024 cold snap, and a local plumber reported more than 180 calls in a day.

That pattern is useful to you. After a hard freeze, check walls and ceilings for new stains once temperatures rise, and learn where your main shutoff is. If the meter spins with everything off, call Missouri American Water at 866-430-0820 to ask who owns the pipe, then bring in a plumber.

Thermographic inspection standards, such as ASTM C1060 for wall insulation, depend on a temperature difference between wet and dry material. That is the reason infrared works well in some conditions and shows nothing in others.

When to call a plumber.

Call when you see a ceiling stain growing, hear water running with fixtures off, find a warm spot on the floor, or see a bill that jumps with no change in use. Do not cut drywall to look. A plumber can usually narrow the spot first.
